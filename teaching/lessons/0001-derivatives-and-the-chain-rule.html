Derivatives, and why the chain rule is the whole trick

Why this lesson exists: Value.backward() in 01-autograd is this rule, applied mechanically at every node in a graph. Nothing in backpropagation is more than what's on this page — the rest is bookkeeping.

A derivative is a sensitivity knob

Forget "slope of the tangent line" for a second. A derivative answers one question: if I nudge the input by a tiny amount, how much does the output move, per unit of nudge?

Formally, for f(x), the derivative at x is

f'(x) = lim (h→0) [f(x+h) − f(x)] / h

Take f(x) = x². Expand (x+h)² − x² = 2xh + h². Divide by h: 2x + h. Let h→0: you're left with 2x. That's the whole derivation — no magic, just algebra and a limit.

This is also exactly what gradcheck.py does numerically, without the limit: pick a tiny h (like 1e-4), compute (f(x+h) − f(x−h)) / 2h, and treat that as "close enough" to the true derivative. That's your ground truth for testing Value.backward().

Primary source: 3Blue1Brown — "The paradox of the derivative" (Essence of Calculus, ch. 2), ~17 min. Watch this if the limit definition feels like symbol-pushing rather than something you can see.

The chain rule: sensitivity through a pipe

Now suppose the output doesn't depend on x directly — it depends on y, which depends on x. Something like x → y → z. How sensitive is z to x?

You multiply the sensitivities along the chain:

dz/dx = dz/dy · dy/dx

That's it. That's backpropagation. If z moves 3 units for every 1-unit move in y, and y moves 2 units for every 1-unit move in x, then z moves 6 units for every 1-unit move in x. Sensitivities compose by multiplication, link by link.

A computational graph is just a longer chain (with branches). Backprop walks it from the output back to the inputs, multiplying local derivatives as it goes, and — the one gotcha worth flagging now — adding them up when a value feeds into more than one place. If a is used twice, its total sensitivity is the sum of its sensitivity through each use. Forget the += here and your engine will silently give wrong gradients for anything that reuses a Value — see the reference doc for the exact rule.

Primary source: 3Blue1Brown — "Visualizing the chain rule and product rule" (Essence of Calculus, ch. 4), ~18 min.

Worked example: the circuit from 01-autograd

This is the exact circuit your gradcheck target uses — a single logistic node, the atomic unit of everything you'll build in 02 and 03: 

z = w1*x1 + w2*x2 + b
p = sigmoid(z)                    where sigmoid(z) = 1 / (1 + e^-z)
L = -[y·log(p) + (1-y)·log(1-p)]  (binary cross-entropy)

Chain rule for dL/dw1: dL/dw1 = dL/dp · dp/dz · dz/dw1. Each link is easy on its own:

  • dz/dw1 = x1 — it's just the coefficient.
  • dp/dz = p·(1-p) — the sigmoid's own derivative (take this as given for now; it falls out of the same limit-definition process as the x² example above).
  • dL/dp = -(y/p) + (1-y)/(1-p) — the BCE loss's derivative w.r.t. its input.

Multiply dL/dp · dp/dz and the algebra collapses to something much simpler than it looks: 

dL/dz = p − y

That's the entire messy chain-rule product, reduced to "prediction minus label." So: 

dL/dw1 = (p - y) * x1
dL/dw2 = (p - y) * x2
dL/db  = (p - y)

If that looks like the gradient of plain linear regression — it is. The sigmoid+cross-entropy pairing is specifically chosen because it produces this clean a gradient; it's not a coincidence you'll see elsewhere in deep learning.
